# Selecting the paper's analyses

Research planning report, September 7, 2026. Scope: the Massachusetts Figure 1–6 family applied to CA, MA and VA, with California major comparisons as supporting evidence. The older California-paper visuals and custom analysis families are outside this selection.

**Build the paper around how coursework becomes useful degree credit.** Figures 3–4 provide the strongest foundation; Figure 2 can explain which disciplines create the mismatch. Figure 1 provides context, while cost and complexity should receive less space unless a carefully reconstructed case adds something the credit measures miss. The intended contribution is a comparison of transfer arrangements and their remaining bottlenecks, supported by modeled pathways and selected source examples.

The investigations support moving into analysis and case selection. They also identify specific definition and source issues to settle before freezing comparative figures. Correct arithmetic within a visual does not establish that different states' visuals measure the same thing. These are curriculum models, not observed student transcripts, graduation rates or causal estimates of state-policy effects.

**Recommended figure set.** Keep three main analytical displays, with a compact coverage overview if space permits. Use the existing MA-family questions, combining redundant displays where useful.

| Priority | Existing visual | Contribution to the paper | Recommended treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Figure 3: associate credit application | Distinguishes degree requirements met from credits merely accepted | Main figure, ideally augmented with named-major, GE, elective and unused-credit allocation. Harmonize the sending plan and accounting definition across states. |
| 2 | Figure 2: course-type coverage | Identifies disciplinary bottlenecks and supports the CA major comparison | Main mechanism figure after MA category reconciliation and a common VA cohort. Show whole-degree and comparable lower-division scopes. |
| 3 | Figure 4: excess credit hours | Translates misalignment into additional coursework | Companion distribution or panel, using the same pathways as Figure 3. Separate unused credit from degree length above the benchmark. |
| 4 | Figure 1: coverage heatmap | Shows where preparation opportunities vary | Compact overview or appendix. Show attainable preparation and its shortfall; preserve the original MA course-observation baseline. |

| 6 | Figure 5: estimated cost | Gives policy context for excess coursework | Appendix or a small annotation. It largely reprices Figure 4 and currently mixes price years and components. |

**GE-inclusive units are appropriate for credit questions, but are not a universal precision upgrade.** Figure 3 already includes applicable GE/breadth credits. A course-type question may deliberately exclude GE so that broad transfer protections do not conceal major-specific problems. Actual course counts, receiving-requirement credit weights, sending credits accepted, and credits applied to graduation are distinct quantities. Fractional semester-equivalent credits can be legitimate after calendar conversion; fractional class counts require an explicit estimation label.

The Figure 2 mismatch is a source-population issue worth resolving before treating it as a decomposition of Figure 1. For example, Lowell's Figure 1 contains four universally available general-elective slots, which Figure 2 omits. Some other differences need row-level adjudication. The advertised GE-excluded MA scope is therefore not a perfectly uniform taxonomy. Preserve the published baselines and create a separately identified common classification. The final source does not contain the revised per-course curricula needed to silently replace these baselines. Full evidence and the bounded recovery plan are in the [MA feasibility report](ma-ge-unit-feasibility.md).

**The most interesting cross-state result is a promising Virginia advantage with an unresolved comparison basis.** Virginia's default catalog model applies 98.47% of guide-plan credits, compared with 67.76% of selected MA associate credits and 59.89% in verified CA CS local-AS models. These values are useful for deciding what to investigate; the differences are not yet estimates of how much better one state's system performs.

| Current source population | Pairs with finite results | Current credit-application measure | What the result represents |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| MA CS, final selected pathways | 61 | 67.76% | Final-paper associate-degree credit fractions; selected nearby pathways |
| CA CS, verified local A.S. sources | 243 of 252 selected | 59.89% | Optimistic modeled plans; 196 estimated and 47 marked `ok` |
| VA CS, default catalog cohort | 240 | 98.47% | Guide application including accepted elective slots; all estimated |

Virginia's numerator includes accepted elective slots in the guide; a strict named/GE counterpart to the MA and CA figures has not been calculated. Its figure also starts from a destination-specific guide intended to fit the destination. It does not independently solve each college's actual AS curriculum as the CA model attempts to do. Its broad-category supply assumptions, course choices and credit ranges also matter. MA's 61 selected nearby pairs, CA's available verified local degrees across nine UC programs, and VA's 16 CS colleges × 15 selected guides are different populations. Calling their raw differences a state ranking would merge these design differences with the policy question.

The proposed mechanism itself has good primary-source support. VCCS describes common curricula developed through repeated collaboration between two- and four-year partners. The official administrative site requires adoption when a college offers the discipline as a major or degree, and lists CS implementation in Fall 2023 with a reviewed Fall 2026 version. That supports testing coordination as an explanation. It does not imply one identical transcript, universal course delivery, or automatic admission to every receiving program. [VCCS collaboration account](https://www.vccs.edu/blog/common-curriculum-pathways-improve-transfer-experience-for-virginia-community-college-students/), [official approved common curricula](https://sites.google.com/email.vccs.edu/tvacollab/cc-and-course-development/tr-cc-disciplines-approved).

Within the VA model, preparation coverage is **50.16% of the whole degree against a 50.39% guide ceiling**: almost all intended pre-transfer preparation is available in the catalog. A low-looking whole-degree percentage can therefore coexist with excellent intended preparation. Public receivers alone give 98.64% guide credit application, so private-university inclusion does not explain the favorable aggregate. Of the default mean 0.958 unused guide credits, 0.667 are explicitly denied and 0.292 are attributed to missing-course substitutes. This suggests a useful distinction between largely coordinated curricula and small receiving-policy refusals.

Two new source findings must precede precise VA unit or scheduling claims. Fourteen of 15 selected guides assign CSC 221 three credits, while all 22 captured catalogs carrying it assign four. This calls for reconciliation of source year, credits earned, credits accepted and receiving requirements waived; it does not automatically mean one more lost credit. The scheduled view drops utilization to 89.49%, but colleges expose different term windows, from Fall-only through Fall/Spring/Summer. That drop is a temporal data sensitivity, not evidence of two-year course shortages. VA Figure 2 also uses 16 canonical BS receiving programs rather than the 15-guide population in Figures 1/3/4. See the [Virginia report](paper-planning-virginia.md) for primary sources, exact program differences, row ambiguities and case candidates.

**California major comparisons provide a strong mechanism test.** Figure 2 shows that the computing gap persists after removing much of the structural upper-division burden:

| CA major's own discipline | Whole-degree course-type coverage | Model's lower-division scope | Universities |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Computer science | 11.54% | 45.70% | 9 |
| Biology | 42.67% | 84.99% | 9 |
| Economics | 15.14% | 91.58% | 9 |

These are equal university means over the same 115 sending colleges. The lower-division field follows model transfer-tier classifications, not a new independent adjudication of every course's level. Nevertheless, it provides a useful robustness check: economics' low whole-degree result largely changes with scope, whereas computing remains substantially lower. This supports investigating programming sequences, languages, course-pair requirements and receiving curricula. It does not by itself establish which of those mechanisms causes the gap.

Receiving-campus variation is also substantial on that identical sending cohort: modeled lower-division computing coverage ranges from **18.43% at Berkeley to 72.61% at Riverside**, a 54.17-point spread. Berkeley's pinned program is EECS, and the programs differ in content. Start by comparing the required coursework and specific equivalencies; do not attribute the difference solely to sending-college quality. In both historical MA and current CA Figure 2 distributions, computing coverage is below math and science. Exact CA/MA magnitude claims should wait for the MA population reconciliation.

**The GE/unit sensitivity itself changes the story.** Across all 1,035 CA college–university pairs per major, the current model yields:

| Measure | CS | Biology | Economics |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Named course observations, GE excluded | 31.92% | 51.18% | 23.73% |
| Named course observations, GE included | 46.04% | 60.67% | 48.09% |
| Template requirement units, GE excluded | 35.26% | 49.95% | 51.00% |
| Template requirement units, GE included | 48.94% | 58.71% | 60.51% |
| Graduation credit budget, GE included | 48.48% | 55.38% | 56.35% |

Do not read these rows as interchangeable estimates of one latent transfer rate. The course population excludes free-elective padding; the unit rollup includes modeled elective capacity; the graduation-budget version additionally applies caps and remaining space. For example, the captured UCSD Economics template includes 62 quarter units of assumed transferable elective capacity that do not constitute 62 units of named economics requirements. Counts also include estimated observations for some opaque blocks. The economics ranking reversal consequently reflects scope and credit-allocation assumptions as well as weighting. Calling it simply “more precise because units” would be misleading.

Biology's advantage over CS is more stable: its GE-excluded course coverage is higher in **946 of 1,035 matched pairs**, and its GE-inclusive template-unit coverage is higher in **881 pairs**, with six ties. These are descriptive comparisons within the captured program set. Economics is especially useful as a contrast showing how small lower-division major demand and elective space affect apparent portability; it currently lacks the local-AS outcome snapshot needed for an equivalent Figure 3 comparison.

**Credit application and excess coursework reveal different burdens.** In the same verified CA CS pathways, application to named/GE requirements averages 59.89%, rising to 67.40% when modeled free-elective capacity counts. The 7.52-point difference is almost as large as the provisional MA–CA strict-application gap. Biology moves from 62.82% to 73.71%, a 10.89-point difference, although all 279 finite verified Biology local-AS results remain estimated and their sources are not marked analysis-ready. Use that result to select cases, not as a settled outcome comparison.

MA's excess-credit mean is **12.92** across the 49 pathways displayed in Figures 4–6, but **15.02** across all 61 pathways available for hours in the final workbook. The omitted 12 average 23.58 excess credits. Verified CA CS averages **19.56 semester-equivalent credits**. The apparent MA–CA gap is therefore 6.64 credits with the printed subset or 4.54 with all 61. Report the denominator and use matched pathways when combining figures. VA's current Figure 4 shows unused guide credits and needs a common definition before joining this comparison.

The final MA sources also offer a useful discordant case: **Holyoke → UMass Amherst has 48.44% strict credit application, zero excess hours and +25 complexity**. That is an interesting research lead because credits, elective accommodation and prerequisite structure need not move together. The final aggregate values are available; the final course-level explanation is not. Recover that pathway before explaining its cause. The [Figures 3–6 memo](paper-planning-ma-figures.md) includes further cases, cohort sensitivities and a draft joint plot.

**Further investigations, in practical order.** Keep the work bounded around claims that can change the paper.

1. **Fix the common measurement contract.** Pin academic year, receiving program/award and institution sector; define the sending population and whether plans are chosen optimally. Use sending credits for the credit-allocation ledger, separate receiving requirement credits waived, normalize calendar units once, and net GE/major overlap. Show strict application and elective-inclusive application separately. For MA, distinguish the 165 availability pairs, 61 credit pathways and 49 complexity pathways.
2. **Reconcile the highest-value sources.** Reclassify MA's 270 Figure 1 columns and category tallies; resolve VA's CSC 221/205 credit conflicts and ambiguous choice rows; review the remaining CA CS template budgets and selected AS course-pair choices. Human source verification alone does not make every model output publication-ready.
3. **Test the policy mechanism with matched pathways.** Build actual VA common-curriculum AS plans under the same destination-aware rules used elsewhere. Within CA, compare local A.S. and A.S.-T on the same sending colleges and receiving campuses. This can test whether transfer-designed curricula reduce avoidable loss without confounding the comparison with different colleges. Treat cross-state differences as descriptive unless a credible policy-timing design is added.
4. **Audit a small set of contrasts.** MA Framingham has 36.37% mean strict application and 33.88 excess credits across eight selected pathways; Dartmouth has 91.70% and zero across three. Examine these alongside Holyoke–Amherst. In CA, compare Berkeley's EECS and a higher-coverage program's exact lower-division requirements. In VA, investigate the denied SDV credits and Virginia Tech's conjunctive CSC 205/215 requirement. Use each as a source question, not a verdict about a school.
5. **Investigate actual availability only after matching time windows.** Capture a common multi-term horizon or documented rotations and check feasible sequences. Add capacity, cross-college or online access where the evidence warrants it. A course absent from one exposed term is insufficient to establish a persistent access failure.

**The best additional visuals are compact explanations of the existing figures.** A credit-allocation bar beside Figure 3 would distinguish major, GE, elective and unused credits, including which portions are assumed. A ceiling-and-shortfall panel beside Figure 1 would separate university-reserved work from missing preparation. The CA scope comparison above strengthens Figure 2. If final graph detail can be recovered, a two-pathway prerequisite diagram could explain why equal credit burden can hide different sequencing constraints. These additions would contribute more than reproducing six large heatmaps for every state.

**Reproducibility and deliverables.** The [planning artifact directory](../analysis/results/paper_planning/) contains frozen input projections, source hashes, cell/campus tables, feasibility inventories and draft plots. The CA Figure 1/2 analysis uses corrected September 7 audit exports, averages underlying course ratios rather than rounded display fields, checks unique complete grids and verifies that the CA category tallies partition the no-GE course observations. MA checks cover all 4,050 final booleans, all 38 Figure 2 plotting values and final credit fractions. VA checks cover all 1,170 stored variants. The Figure 3–6 script checks final-workbook values against published rounded cells. No new application, source-data or database changes were made during this planning work.
