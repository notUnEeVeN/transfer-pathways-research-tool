ENGL 185  - Critical Reading and Writing  (4 Hours)  
This course provides an intensive introduction to all of the skills that go into good writing: critical reading, framing arguments for different audiences, mechanics, style, and research. The seminar must be taken during a student's first year at the College. The core curriculum will ask students to continue to refine their writing, but this course lays the foundation for the kinds of writing expected of students throughout college.
ENGL 202  - Midnight Tales  (3 Hours)  
Ambrose Bierce once defined the ghost as "an outward and visible sign of an invisible fear." He might have said the same of any number of other supernatural creatures: vampires, zombies, werewolves, that thing you thought you saw out of the corner of your eye the other night when you were up late and the house was quiet . . . . But are all of these creatures manifestations of the same fear? What is it we are so afraid of, anyway? These are questions we will attempt to answer in this course, through reading, discussing, and writing about a range of horrifying poems, short stories, and novels. We will also practice our close reading skills and become familiar with literary terminology and critical approaches to reading.
Curriculum: HC,HU,WA
ENGL 203  - Boys Don't Cry  (3 Hours)  
This course examines the diverse (and sometimes conflicting) representations of masculinity in American literary culture. Taking as its material both "classic" and contemporary texts, this course aims to introduce students to the ways in which masculinity has been represented by both male and female authors, exploring not only how authors construct notions of masculinity based on the social and historical circumstances that surrounded them, but also how these notions continue to affect our present-day understanding of what it means to "be a man." In this course, we work to expose the interpretive possibilities contained in even the most seemingly straightforward depictions of "manliness" (such as in texts like Hemingway's "Hills like White Elephants") while also searching for more subtle explorations of alternative masculinities. Along the way, we'll ask key questions about what have long been considered to be the developmental "touchstones" of a masculine identity: how is boyhood and masculine adolescence represented in literary culture? How are men depicted against backdrops of violence and war? How are men represented as they navigate relationships (familial, friendly, and romantic)? Students in this course will have the unique opportunity to be introduced to the work of textual interpretation through a cultural and theoretical lens, all the while investigating—and challenging—their own notions of masculinity.
Curriculum: HC,HU,WA
ENGL 204  - Reading Disability  (3 Hours)  
This course takes disability's pervasiveness in literature—Oedipus's blindness, Richard III's humpback, Ahab's peg leg—as an opportunity to examine the meaning of disability itself. While it is easy to assume that "disability" refers only to a scientific category or medical diagnosis, reading literary texts that concentrate on disabled experience reveals how disability inevitably connects to cultural and historical concepts that have equal bearing on our perceptions of it. In fact, the cultural connotations surrounding disability often condition how it is treated within the medical community. In surveying a wide variety of literary texts, this course will ask students to analyze the historical variations in disability's meaning with the ultimate aim of contesting simplistic divisions between able-bodied (or "normal") and disabled ("abnormal") individuals.
Curriculum: DI,HC,HU,WA
ENGL 205  - Dangerous Women: Romance Book Heroines in the Modern Age  (3 Hours)  
Visit any bookstore today and you’ll see--right at the front, with pride of place--tables full of brightlycolored paperbacks with romantic, sometimes punny titles, and couples embracing on the front. This is the time of the explosively popular modern romance novel, and the distance between it and the early days of this genre’s development could not be more pronounced. But the critically maligned and publicly scorned romance novels of the late twentieth century form the foundation for the explosive popularity of the genre we see today. In this course, we focus specifically on the romance heroine as a means of exploring the rise of the modern romance genre over the last fifty years, considering the social and critical reception of these books, the politics of gender, sexuality, race, identity, and more contained within them, and the market trends and market realities that have driven it to its latest heights.
Prerequisite(s): ENGL 185
Curriculum: AE,HU,WA
ENGL 206  - Alternative Richmond  (3 Hours)  
This is an alternative travel course focusing on Richmond’s alternative culture. As an “alternative travel course,” this course applies the same general method used by any travel course--site-based experiences that lead to intensive engagements with place--to a location close to home. In this case, the location is Richmond. Through classroom study, conversations with local professionals, guided workshops, and scheduled daytrips to Richmond, students in this course will explore the city’s “alternative culture,” which signifies the kind of art and culture taking place outside of the mainstream. The course will primarily conduct this engagement through the study of literature, but it also casts a wider net as it ambles through different versions of Richmond’s “alt” scene: Edgar Allen Poe’s gothic from the nineteenth century; James Brach Cabel’s weird fiction from the turn of the twentieth century; the punk zines from the late twentieth century; and local band GWAR’s heavy metal from the turn of this last century.
Curriculum: AE,HU,EL

ENGL 333  - African American Modernism  (3 Hours)

ENGL 337  - Poetry! Politics! Protest!  (3 Hours)  
How can we use poetry to make something happen? This course addresses this question by conducting a survey of contemporary poets who are especially interested in applying their writing to causes of activism and social justice. We can think here of poetry collections like Solmaz Sharif’s Look (2016), which forces us to confront the Arab immigrant experience in post-9/11 America, and Claudia Rankine’s Citizen (2014), which, among other things, tackles the way people of color like Zinedine Zidane and Sarena Williams are portrayed in the media. In drawing on such voices, this course highlights diversity as an integral part of contemporary poetry. But even more importantly, it examines how poetry (and the conversations we have around poetry) can serve as a means of articulating the equity and accessibility issues central to various social justice movements Disability Rights, #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, and others). In other words, if political struggle has often been understood through the language of “finding a voice,” this course introduces students to the ways poetry can be instrumental in our political consciousness and selfreflection.
Prerequisite(s): ENGL 185
Curriculum: DI
ENGL 339  - Genre Fiction  (3 Hours)  
Genre Fiction is an introduction to popular historic and contemporary genre literature – the types of books frequently found in grocery stores, airports, and best sellers lists, and only very rarely found in the college classroom. Romances, science fiction novels, detective stories, fantasy epics, and horror stories may be snubbed as escapist, "low-brow" literature, but the pleasures these texts yield reveal much about contemporary culture. By scrutinizing genre fiction with the same academic rigor we apply to "great" literature, we will try to define a variety of popular genres and come to an understanding of what makes these genres – and the specific texts we will read – so appealing. Group I.
Prerequisite(s): Not open to new students
ENGL 340  - The Neurodiverse Novel  (3 Hours)  
This course uses the novel to explore "neurodiversity," or the idea that all minds process the world around us differently. The course focuses on the narrative strategies authors use to depict various cognitive disabilities and mental illnesses (autism, down syndrome, and schizophrenia, among others) while also aiming to cultivate an appreciation for the novel as an art form that helps us to empathize with the minds of others. Group I.
Prerequisite(s): Not open to new freshman

ENGL 356  - Dublin, Literary Capital  (3 Hours)  
Dublin is the backdrop of much Irish literature and a center of cultural life in Europe. In 2010, UNESCO--the educational branch of the United Nations--even named Dublin an official "city of literature." This course gives students the opportunity to closely examine this important literary capital. In addition to analyzing literary works that feature Dublin's literary scene, students will have the chance to engage in first-hand exploration of the city through a study-abroad trip that focuses on Ireland's literary heritage. Site visits will include such places as James Joyce's Tower, the W.B. Yeats exhibit at the National Library of Ireland, and Paleolithic ruins at Newgrange. Visiting these sites will provide students with added insight into the literature we read for class while also enabling them to reflect critically upon how Dublin has built an international reputation and major tourist economy around its literary resources. Group I.
Prerequisite(s): Not open to new freshman
Curriculum: EL

ENGL 371  - Toni Morrison's Communities  (3 Hours)  
Toni Morrison, the U.S.'s first African American recipient of the Nobel Prize in literature (1993), locates her work in vibrant and turbulent periods of U.S. and world history including the Great War, U.S. slavery, Reconstruction, the Jazz Age, the Harlem Renaissance, and the Civil Rights Movement. Through a focus on Morrison's novels, beginning with her early works The Bluest Eye and Sula, then Jazz, Tar Baby, Beloved, and Song of Solomon, the course will examine her understanding of history and whether fiction and imagination may safely participate in an objective formulation of the past. Since Morrison's view of U.S. society begins with an understanding of community, the course will investigate her historical understanding of various communities, including at the local and national level, and how ideas of geography, nation, migration, and memory affect her artistic vision. The course will also examine how Morrison's work engages race and gender in the U.S., both separately, and together. Group I.
Prerequisite(s): Not open to new students

This course explores crucial questions about how our culture defines gender, and, more specifically, how literary texts represent gender—specifically, how they define what is "feminine." Students will practice the work of literary criticism through the lens of feminist/gender theory. Group I.

ENGL 495  - Capstone Seminar  (3 Hours)  
An intensive study of an author or topic that culminates in a major research paper. As the English major capstone, the seminar provides a culminating experience in which students will widely integrate, extend, critique, and apply knowledge and skills from the student's major program. Group I.
Prerequisite(s): junior standing
Curriculum: CS,EL
ENGL 496  - Senior Project  (3 Hours)  
The preparation and oral defense of a lengthy thesis in the field of British or American literature. Open only to seniors. Departmental approval is required.
